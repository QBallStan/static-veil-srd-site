
## Time Die

When it matters exactly how long something takes, the GM uses a **Time Die**: a d6 multiplied by a unit of time, such as seconds, minutes, hours or days.

Only roll it when timing matters, such as with guards on patrol or a deadline coming. Otherwise, use 4.

| Time Die | Example |
|---|---|
| d6 seconds | Bypassing a simple physical obstacle |
| d6 rounds | Disarming a device during combat |
| d6 × 10 seconds | Picking a basic lock |
| d6 × 30 seconds | Forcing open a jammed mechanism |
| d6 minutes | Applying first aid |
| d6 × 10 minutes | Repairing damaged equipment |
| d6 × 30 minutes | Thoroughly searching a building |
| d6 hours | Performing extensive technical work |
| d6 × 4 hours | Researching a difficult subject |
| d6 × 10 hours | Overhauling complex machinery |
| d6 days | Decoding a large archive |
| d6 weeks | Completing a major long-term project |

Some rules give an action its own Time Die.

### Rushing and Taking Time

Before rolling the Time Die, a character can change their pace.

- **Rushing** halves the time and gives −20 to the [[Success Targets and Hits|Success Target]].
- **Taking Time** doubles the time and gives +20 to the Success Target.

Roll the Time Die, then halve or double the result. Round to the nearest sensible unit. A rushed task measured in rounds still takes at least 1 round.

## Extended Tests

Some tasks are too big or too tense for a single roll, like defusing a bomb, breaking into a secure server, or getting people out of a burning building before the roof comes down. An **Extended Test** gives the characters a set number of intervals to build up [[Success Targets and Hits#Hits|Hits]] before time runs out.

The GM decides what the task is, how long each interval lasts, how many intervals the characters have, and how many Hits they need. An interval can be a fixed length of time or a Time Die.

### One Character

When only one character can work on the task at a time, use these guidelines:

| Task | Hits | Intervals | Examples |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Challenging | 4 | 3 | Defusing a simple explosive, bypassing a cheap keypad lock |
| Difficult | 8 | 4 | Defusing a booby-trapped bomb, performing a ritual |
| Complex | 15 | 5 | Breaking into a hardened corporate system, repairing complex machinery |

### Several Characters

When several characters can work at once, assume each one makes about 2 Hits per interval. That gives a fair task. Raise or lower the total to make it harder or easier. For example, three characters with 4 intervals to restore power before the backup fails need about 24 Hits.

Set the total when the task starts. If more characters join later, they just help finish sooner.

### Counting What's Saved

Sometimes Hits measure how much the characters achieve, not whether they win or lose. When they're carrying victims out of a fire or grabbing data before a server wipes, each Hit is one person saved or one file recovered. The GM decides whether it's possible to get everything.

### Making Progress

In each interval, every character working on the task makes a test. Add the Hits from successful tests to the total. Failed tests add nothing. If the total reaches the required Hits in time, the task succeeds. If not, it fails.

### Complications

A [[Complications|Complication]] during an Extended Test causes a setback, but it doesn't wipe out progress unless the situation really calls for it. A tool might break, someone might notice, or the job might eat through supplies.

### Partial Success

If an Extended Test ends short of the required Hits, the GM can still allow a partial result when the progress justifies it. The characters might recover part of the data, delay a security response instead of stopping it, or patch up a machine so it holds for now.
