
Characters interact with **[[The Net]]** through a variety of interfaces, ranging from ordinary augmented reality to full neural immersion.

Most people rely on a **[[Interfaces#PADs|Personal Access Device (PAD)]]** and augmented reality for everyday use. Virtual reality provides immersive access to specific services and environments, while hackers and other specialists may use **[[Interfaces#Deep Interface|Deep Interface]]** to interact with network structures at much greater speed and precision.

The interface used does not change the underlying architecture of The Net. It changes how information is presented to the user and, in some cases, what actions they can perform.

### PADs

A **[[Interfaces#PADs|Personal Access Device (PAD)]]** is the standard interface used to connect to **[[The Net]]**.

A PAD typically handles a character's communications, identity, payments, credentials, augmented reality, personal data, and access to connected equipment. It also acts as the central interface for most [[The Net#Personal Networks|personal networks]].

PADs come in many forms, including handheld devices, wrist units, badges, pendants, and implanted systems. Their physical form does not significantly change how they function in the rules.

Most characters are assumed to own and carry a PAD unless their circumstances say otherwise.

A PAD may connect to weapons, augmentations, sensors, vehicles, and other personal equipment. When it does, those devices normally form part of the character's **personal network** and are treated as a single hacking target.

### Augmented Reality

**Augmented Reality (AR)** is the standard way most people interact with **[[The Net]]**.

AR overlays digital information onto the physical world through lenses, glasses, implants, PAD displays, or other compatible interfaces. It is used for navigation, communication, device controls, service access, advertisements, work instructions, permissions, warnings, and other everyday functions.

AR does not place the user inside a virtual environment. The character remains fully aware of and able to act in the physical world while using it.

Most ordinary Net interactions are assumed to occur through AR unless another interface is specified.

### Virtual Reality

**Virtual Reality (VR)** is a fully immersive interface that projects the user's consciousness into **[[The Net]]** and replaces their physical senses with a simulated sensory environment.

When entering VR, a user normally appears within their personal **VR Hub**. A Hub is a private interface environment used to organize services, contacts, applications, and destinations. It can be customized into virtually any form, from a familiar room or landscape to a gothic cathedral, spacecraft, abstract structure, or featureless void.

From their Hub, users navigate menus, links, and other interfaces to access VR-enabled **[[The Net#Hosts|Hosts]]**. Each Host determines the environment and sensations it provides.

VR can reproduce sight, sound, touch, taste, smell, balance, motion, temperature, pain, and other sensations where supported by the Host. A virtual meal can have texture and flavor without providing nourishment, while entertainment services can reproduce physical experiences that would be impossible or impractical outside VR.

VR can also relay the sensory experience of another person or device when an appropriate interface is available. A user connected to an augmented racing driver, for example, could experience the race from the driver's perspective and receive a simulation of the sensations the driver is experiencing.

Commercial VR normally employs **sensory limiters** that prevent simulated sensations from reaching their full physical intensity. The experience can approach reality closely, but ordinary systems deliberately restrict dangerous levels of pain, disorientation, acceleration, and other potentially harmful stimuli.

VR is not a continuous virtual world or a representation of the physical architecture of The Net. Moving between VR Hosts means connecting to different hosted services, not travelling through a shared digital landscape.

Accessing a Host through VR also does not bypass the underlying network route used to reach it. **[[The Net#Shards|Shard]]** distance and other connection restrictions still apply normally.
### Deep Interface

**[[Interfaces#Deep Interface|Deep Interface]]** is the administrative and technical VR layer through which the underlying systems of **[[The Net]]** can be directly observed and manipulated.

Where ordinary VR presents information and services to users, Deep Interface exposes the infrastructure beneath them: connections, routing, permissions, security systems, data flows, active processes, and other technical structures. It is the plumbing inside the walls rather than the finished room.

Deep Interface translates this complexity into a representation that an operator can understand and manipulate at high speed. Routing paths might appear as roads, currents, tunnels, or branching structures. Firewalls and defensive Scripts may appear as walls, creatures, machinery, or other obstacles. The exact presentation depends on the operator, their hardware, their software, and the systems being accessed.

Deep Interface is not a literal geography of The Net. Its visual structures represent underlying systems and relationships rather than physical locations. Moving through a representation does not bypass **Shards**, shorten network distance, or change the actual route between the operator and a target.

Accessing Deep Interface places significant strain on the nervous system. Diving without equipment designed to regulate the neural load is dangerous and can cause severe injury. Hackers therefore normally use specialized **Rigs** or **Cold Cradles** when entering Deep Interface.
### Rigs and Cold Cradles

A **Rig** is specialized hacking hardware used to access **[[Interfaces#Deep Interface|Deep Interface]]** and perform advanced operations on **[[The Net]]**. It provides the processing power, memory, security systems, and neural interface required for serious hacking.

Most Rigs are portable enough to be used in the field. Entering Deep Interface through a Rig without additional protective equipment is called a **Hot Dive**.

A **Cold Cradle** is a separate full-immersion support system designed to make Diving safer. It regulates neural load, monitors the operator's condition, provides cooling and body support, and helps absorb dangerous feedback.

A Cold Cradle does not normally replace a Rig. The hacker connects their Rig to the Cradle and uses both together. Some high-end models combine a Rig and Cold Cradle into a single integrated unit.

Entering Deep Interface while supported by a Cold Cradle is called a **Cold Dive**. Cold Diving provides the same access and speed as a Hot Dive, but reduces the physical consequences of neural feedback.
### Hardlines

A **Hardline** is a direct physical connection between a Rig and a device, Host access point, or other network infrastructure.

Hardline access bypasses the need to reach the target through intervening **[[The Net#Shards|Shards]]** and provides a more stable, direct route for intrusion.

A Hardline does not bypass the target's **[[Hacking#Hacking Defense|Firewall]]**, grant [[Access and Authority#Keys|Keys]], or otherwise provide automatic authority. The hacker must still overcome the target's security normally.

Hardlines are especially useful against isolated systems, heavily segmented Hosts, or targets that cannot be reached wirelessly.

A hacker may enter Deep Interface while connected through a Hardline. This is still a **Hot Dive** or **Cold Dive** depending on whether a **Cold Cradle** is being used. Hardline describes the connection method, not a separate type of Dive.

Using a Hardline physically ties the hacker to the point of entry until the connection is broken.
